Vertical gardens and living walls: what actually works

By Garden.City Editorial · Updated October 2026 · 7 min read

When floor space is the limit, walls and railings become the garden. Growing up can double or triple the planting area of a balcony or courtyard. But vertical systems are also where many beginners fail, because small pockets of soil hung on a wall dry out at astonishing speed. Here’s how to pick a system that suits your space and your schedule.

The five main approaches

1. Climbers on a trellis

The simplest and most reliable vertical garden is a normal, deep container at floor level with a climbing plant trained up a trellis, mesh or strings. Pole beans, peas, compact cucumbers, climbing nasturtiums, jasmine and clematis all work. Because the roots sit in a large volume of soil, watering stays easy. Start here if you’re new.

2. Railing planters and tiered shelves

Troughs that hook over a railing, or ladder-style shelves holding several pots, keep each plant in its own container. They’re easy to rearrange and replace. Confirm that railing planters are allowed and securely fixed, and keep shelves stable against wind.

3. Fabric pocket planters

Felt pockets mounted on a wall are cheap and attractive at first, but each pocket holds very little mix and wicks moisture to the air. In sun or wind they may need watering daily or more. They work best in shade, with drip irrigation, for herbs, strawberries and ferns.

4. Stacking towers

Stackable pots and tower planters place many plants above a shared column of soil, often watered from the top so moisture trickles down. They suit strawberries, lettuce and herbs and use very little floor space. Turn them weekly so all sides get light.

5. Modular living walls

Professional living walls use rigid modules or panels with built-in irrigation, sometimes with recirculating water and fertiliser dosing. Indoors they bring dramatic greenery to lobbies and offices; outdoors they can insulate and cool walls. They’re impressive but carry real cost and maintenance obligations, and must be fixed to a structurally suitable wall with a moisture barrier to protect the building. This is a job for a specialist installer.

Rule of thumb: the smaller the soil volume per plant, the more you’ll rely on automatic irrigation. Budget for a drip kit and timer from day one if you choose pockets or wall panels.

Choosing plants for vertical spaces

  • Edible: strawberries, lettuce, spinach, chives, thyme, oregano, mint (contained), and climbing beans or peas on trellis.
  • Ornamental, sun: sedums, trailing nasturtium, small ornamental grasses.
  • Ornamental, shade or indoor: ferns, pothos, philodendron, heuchera.

Put thirstier plants near the bottom of any system watered from the top, and drought-tolerant herbs near the top.

Watering and maintenance

Install a drip line with one emitter per pocket or module and a battery timer on the tap. Use a quality mix with extra coir or compost to hold moisture. Place a drip tray or gravel strip below to protect floors and neighbours. Feed lightly but often, because frequent watering flushes nutrients out. Replace tired plants each spring.

Common mistakes

  • Mounting pockets on a hot, sunny wall without irrigation.
  • Fixing heavy systems to walls that weren’t assessed for the load.
  • Letting water run down the building façade.
  • Choosing large, vigorous plants for tiny pockets.

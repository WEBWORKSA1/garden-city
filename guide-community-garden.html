How to start a community garden

By Garden.City Editorial · Updated October 2026 · 8 min read

Community gardens turn vacant lots, school corners and unused church grounds into shared places to grow food, meet neighbours and learn. They’re also projects with real responsibilities: land agreements, water bills, insurance and people who need to get along. This checklist walks through the steps most successful gardens follow.

Step 1: Organise a core group

No garden survives on one enthusiast. Recruit five to ten committed people before you look at land. Hold an open meeting (a library room or café works), collect contact details and ask what people want: individual plots, a shared food garden, a children’s area, a pollinator patch? Assign early roles — coordinator, treasurer, outreach, site lead — even if informal.

Step 2: Find and secure land

Look for sites with at least six hours of sun, access to water, reasonable public access and a cooperative owner. Possibilities include municipal parks departments, schools, places of worship, housing authorities, hospitals and private owners of vacant lots. Many cities run community-garden programs that offer land and support — start by contacting your parks or urban agriculture office.

Get a written agreement covering how long you can use the land, who pays for water, what you may build, liability, and what happens if the owner needs the site back. Multi-year terms make investment worthwhile.

Step 3: Test the soil and plan water

Urban soils may contain lead and other contaminants from old paint, traffic or past industry. Send samples to a soil-testing lab before planting food in the ground. If results are poor, build raised beds with imported soil over a barrier layer. Confirm where water will come from — a metered hydrant connection, a neighbouring building or rain harvesting — and who pays for it.

Don’t skip soil testing on any former urban lot. It protects gardeners, especially children.

Step 4: Write rules and arrange insurance

Clear, short rules prevent most conflicts. Cover plot fees and assignment, required work hours, what may be grown, pesticide policy, tool use, composting, keeping paths clear, abandoned plots and how disputes are resolved. Have every member sign them.

Landowners usually require liability insurance. Options include coverage through a sponsoring nonprofit, a municipal program or a community-garden network that offers group policies.

Step 5: Fund, design and build

Typical start-up costs are lumber or beds, soil, a water connection, fencing, a tool shed, compost bins and signage. Combine modest plot fees with local business sponsorships, neighbourhood grants, crowdfunding and in-kind donations of materials. Design with accessible paths and some raised beds at wheelchair height. Plan a community build day — it creates ownership from the start.

Keeping it going

  • Hold a season-opening and harvest celebration every year.
  • Keep a waiting list and reassign neglected plots promptly.
  • Share surplus with a local food bank.
  • Run workshops: composting, seed saving, pest control.
  • Document everything — photos and harvest totals help with grants.
